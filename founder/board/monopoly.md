# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The product is a distribution channel.** Every paid greeting gets opened by a recipient who did not choose Kitoo, on the phone, in an emotional moment. That is the only structural advantage here. A recipient who becomes a sender is the cheapest customer the business will ever get.
- **The build is done, so it costs little to test.** A solo developer with a working Laravel/React stack and QRIS payments can run experiments for a small budget. A slow start will not kill it.
- **Some features are hard to copy quickly.** Time locks, a mic-driven candle, quizzes, scratch cards, a per-guest invitation link and RSVP plus a gift envelope add up to a lot of work. A weekend copycat cannot match the full set, even if each piece on its own is easy.
- **The planned group mode is the only network effect on the table.** If many senders contribute to one greeting, each sender pulls in others. That is the closest thing to a moat in the plan.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No way to reach buyers, and a market that is probably crowded.** How customers will find Kitoo is "not decided yet". The founder names Lovio.id as a direct comparable. Digital wedding invitations sent over WhatsApp look, by the founder's own category list, like a familiar product type. If that is right, Kitoo enters as one more vendor in a price fight. Competitor prices and the competitor count are unknown (/founder-competitors).
2. **There is no secret and nothing proprietary.** Templates plus interactive widgets are an incremental improvement. They are not ten times better than Lovio or a free Canva-plus-WhatsApp workaround. "More categories" is easy to match. By month six a competitor can ship the same categories.
3. **It tries to own everything instead of something.** About 20 categories across two buyer types (gift-givers and event hosts) means Kitoo starts with a small share of a big, contested space. It should start with a big share of a small one. The wedding segment alone probably has its own established competitors, and spreading effort across all of them dilutes any brand.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single moment can Kitoo be the default for within 12 months? (For example: "nembak" and anniversary greetings for 18–25-year-olds, or aqiqah and khitanan invitations.)
- What do you know about this buyer that Lovio does not?
- How do the first 100 paying customers arrive, and what does each one cost? (/founder-cfo for CAC tolerance, /founder-consumer for intent.)
- What share of recipients click through to make their own? Is there a visible "made with Kitoo" link on every page?
- Who else sells this within reach of the same buyer, and at what price? (/founder-competitors)
- What stops Lovio from copying the time lock, quiz and group mode in one quarter?
- How big is the beachhead segment, in buyers per year? (/founder-competitors, /founder-consumer)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Pick one beachhead.** Choose one or two moment categories and aim to be the clear leader there. Hold back the other categories in marketing (they can stay in the catalog) until that segment is won.
2. **Make the recipient loop measurable.** Add a "make one back" step to the closing/reply screen and track the recipient-to-sender rate from day one. Treat it as the core metric next to draft → paid.
3. **Map the competition before spending.** Run /founder-competitors to price and map Lovio and the invitation players. Show one gap Kitoo can own that is not just "cheaper".
4. **Commit to group mode as the moat.** Commit to it on a dated plan rather than leaving it as a someday feature. It is the only path here to a network effect.
